\begin{abstract}
Automatic image captioning requires the joint modeling of visual content and natural language, making the choice of visual encoder a critical architectural decision. This paper presents a controlled comparative analysis of two encoder-decoder image captioning systems that share an identical Transformer decoder but differ in their visual encoder: one employs a pretrained ResNet-50 convolutional neural network and the other uses a pretrained Vision Transformer (ViT-B/16). Both models are trained and evaluated on the Flickr30k benchmark dataset using standard caption quality metrics, including BLEU-1 through BLEU-4, METEOR, ROUGE-L, and CIDEr. The ViT-based model achieves marginally higher BLEU-4 (0.1679 vs.\ 0.1641) and CIDEr (0.3872 vs.\ 0.3868) scores, suggesting a modest improvement in global semantic coverage. However, the ResNet-based model converges in 172 minutes over 19 epochs, compared with 439 minutes over 21 epochs for ViT, representing a 2.5$\times$ reduction in training time. Inference latency is comparable for both models ($\approx$41--42\,ms per image). These results indicate that ViT encoders marginally outperform CNN encoders on global captioning quality, while CNN encoders offer substantially greater data and computational efficiency on moderate-scale datasets. This trade-off has direct implications for deploying image captioning in resource-constrained environments.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Image captioning is a challenging multimodal task that aims to automatically generate natural language descriptions for images by combining techniques from Computer Vision (CV) and Natural Language Processing (NLP). Unlike traditional image classification or object detection, image captioning requires a system to understand not only the objects present in an image but also their relationships, attributes, and contextual information in order to produce coherent and grammatically correct sentences. The ability to automatically describe visual scenes has become increasingly important due to its applications in assistive technologies for visually impaired individuals, intelligent image retrieval, autonomous vehicles, surveillance systems, robotics, social media content analysis, and human-computer interaction.

Early image captioning systems relied on handcrafted visual features combined with template-based or retrieval-based sentence generation. The rapid advancement of deep learning introduced encoder-decoder architectures inspired by neural machine translation, in which Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) encode visual representations and Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), particularly Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, generate captions sequentially.

Despite remarkable improvements, RNN-based decoders suffer from difficulties in capturing long-range dependencies and cannot be fully parallelized during training. The Transformer architecture addressed these limitations by replacing recurrence with self-attention mechanisms that enable efficient modeling of global dependencies. Transformer-based decoders have since become the dominant approach in modern image captioning systems.

CNN architectures such as ResNet remain highly effective at learning hierarchical local features and are widely adopted as image encoders due to strong inductive biases for translation equivariance and locality. Vision Transformers (ViTs) have emerged as a powerful alternative by applying self-attention directly to sequences of image patches, enabling explicit modeling of long-range spatial dependencies. While ViTs have demonstrated state-of-the-art performance across many vision tasks, they generally require larger datasets and more computational resources than CNN-based models.

This study presents a controlled comparative analysis of two image captioning architectures employing a common Transformer decoder while differing only in their visual encoder. The first architecture uses a pretrained ResNet-50 encoder; the second employs a pretrained ViT-B/16 encoder. Using the Flickr30k benchmark dataset, both models are evaluated on standard caption quality metrics including BLEU-4, METEOR, ROUGE-L, and CIDEr, along with training time, convergence behavior, and inference latency. By isolating the effect of the visual encoder, this work provides a comprehensive assessment of the trade-offs between CNN-based local feature extraction and Vision Transformer-based global contextual representation.

The major contributions of this paper are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item A comparative implementation of ResNet-50 and ViT-B/16 visual encoders paired with an identical Transformer decoder under matched training conditions.
    \item Quantitative evaluation on Flickr30k using BLEU-1--4, METEOR, ROUGE-L, and CIDEr metrics on 1,000 test images.
    \item Comparative analysis of computational efficiency, including encoder size, convergence rate, total training time, and inference latency.
    \item A discussion of the practical trade-offs between CNN local feature extraction and ViT global contextual modeling for image captioning.
\end{itemize}

\section{Literature Review}

Image captioning integrates computer vision and natural language processing to automatically generate coherent textual descriptions of visual content \cite{almalla2026}. The field has evolved from early encoder-decoder models through attention-augmented architectures to recent vision-language foundation models \cite{abdulgalil2025}.

Vinyals \textit{et al.} introduced the Neural Image Caption (NIC) model, establishing the encoder-decoder paradigm by coupling a pretrained GoogLeNet encoder with an LSTM decoder \cite{vinyals2015}. Xu \textit{et al.} extended this framework with soft attention, enabling the decoder to focus on spatially relevant image regions at each generation step \cite{xu2015}. Anderson \textit{et al.} further improved caption quality by incorporating bottom-up attention over Faster R-CNN object proposals, achieving state-of-the-art performance on MS COCO \cite{anderson2018}.

Vaswani \textit{et al.} introduced the Transformer, replacing sequential recurrence with multi-head self-attention to enable fully parallelized training and better modeling of long-range dependencies \cite{vaswani2017}. Transformer decoders rapidly superseded LSTM-based generators in image captioning by producing more fluent and contextually consistent captions. He \textit{et al.} introduced ResNet, addressing the degradation problem in very deep CNNs via residual skip connections, making ResNet-50 one of the most widely adopted visual encoders for captioning tasks \cite{he2016}.

A major paradigm shift occurred with the Vision Transformer (ViT) of Dosovitskiy \textit{et al.}, which partitions images into fixed-size patches and applies Transformer self-attention across the patch sequence, enabling direct modeling of global contextual relationships \cite{dosovitskiy2021}. ViTs demonstrated competitive or superior image classification performance while exhibiting strong scalability with data and model size.

More recently, large-scale vision-language pretraining has pushed captioning performance substantially higher. BLIP \cite{li2022} introduced a bootstrapping strategy that unifies understanding and generation by filtering noisy web image-text pairs. BLIP-2 \cite{li2023} extended this approach by bridging frozen image encoders and frozen large language models (LLMs) via a lightweight Querying Transformer (Q-Former), enabling highly parameter-efficient multimodal pretraining. Zhu \textit{et al.} demonstrated that BLIP-2 can serve as a vision question-answering backbone in an interactive captioning pipeline (ChatCaptioner), identifying 53\% more objects than BLIP-2 alone and producing significantly more informative descriptions \cite{zhu2023}. Despite the impressive performance of these Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs), hallucination remains a critical challenge: models can generate plausible-sounding but factually incorrect descriptions of visual content. Li \textit{et al.} proposed the POPE benchmark specifically to evaluate object hallucination in large vision-language models, revealing that even state-of-the-art MLLMs systematically hallucinate objects not present in images \cite{li2023pope}. These findings emphasize the need to understand how different visual encoders contribute to grounding accuracy in caption generation.

Although ViTs have demonstrated strong representational capabilities, relatively few studies have performed fully controlled comparisons in which only the visual encoder differs while the Transformer decoder, vocabulary, training strategy, dataset, and evaluation protocol remain identical. Such controlled comparisons are essential for isolating the contribution of the visual feature extraction mechanism. Specifically, because ViT relies entirely on learned patch embeddings without the locality and translation-equivariance inductive biases built into CNNs, it is expected to model global scene semantics more effectively but to be less data-efficient than ResNet on moderate-scale datasets such as Flickr30k. Quantifying this trade-off under a controlled experimental design constitutes the central research gap addressed by this study.

\section{Methodology}

Both captioning systems adopt an encoder-decoder framework in which a visual encoder extracts image representations and a shared Transformer decoder generates captions autoregressively. The two systems differ solely in the visual encoder; all other components are identical.

\subsection{CNN-Based Captioning Model}

The CNN encoder is a pretrained ResNet-50 \cite{he2016} with the final classification layer removed. The spatial feature map from the penultimate global average pooling layer produces a 2{,}048-dimensional vector, which is projected to a 512-dimensional embedding via a fully connected layer. This 512-dim vector serves as the memory for the Transformer decoder.

\subsection{Vision Transformer-Based Captioning Model}

The ViT encoder is a pretrained ViT-B/16 \cite{dosovitskiy2021} that divides each $224\times224$ input image into $14\times14 = 196$ non-overlapping $16\times16$ patches. Each patch is linearly embedded and processed by 12 Transformer encoder layers with hidden dimension 768 and 12 attention heads. The classification token (CLS) output, which aggregates global scene information, produces a 768-dimensional representation that is projected to 512 dimensions via a linear layer.

\subsection{Shared Transformer Decoder}

Both models use an identical caption decoder comprising:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A learned token embedding matrix of shape $|\mathcal{V}| \times 512$, where $|\mathcal{V}|=2{,}004$.
    \item A learned positional encoding of shape $128 \times 512$.
    \item A two-layer Transformer decoder with 8 attention heads and model dimension $d_{\text{model}}=512$.
    \item A linear output projection from 512 to $|\mathcal{V}|$.
\end{itemize}
The decoder is trained with causal (autoregressive) masking and cross-attends to the encoder's projected feature vector as the memory context.

\section{Experimental Setup}

\subsection{Dataset}

Both models are trained and evaluated on the Flickr30k dataset \cite{young2014}, which contains 31{,}014 images each annotated with five reference captions. The standard Karpathy split is used: 29{,}000 training images, 1{,}014 validation images, and 1{,}000 test images.

\subsection{Vocabulary}

A shared vocabulary is constructed from the training captions using word-level tokenization. The 2{,}000 most frequent words are retained, yielding a vocabulary of 2{,}004 tokens including four special tokens: \texttt{<PAD>} (index 0), \texttt{<UNK>} (index 1), \texttt{<SOS>} (index 2), and \texttt{<EOS>} (index 3).

\subsection{Image Preprocessing}

All images are resized to $224\times224$ pixels and normalized using ImageNet statistics (mean $[0.485, 0.456, 0.406]$, standard deviation $[0.229, 0.224, 0.225]$). No data augmentation is applied during training.

\subsection{Training Configuration}

Both models are optimized using Adam with a differential learning rate schedule: the pretrained encoder backbone is fine-tuned at $1\times10^{-5}$ while the projection head and the Transformer decoder are trained at $1\times10^{-4}$. The loss function is cross-entropy with padding positions ignored. Training uses a batch size of 16, a maximum of 100 epochs, and early stopping with patience of 5 epochs and a minimum improvement threshold of $1\times10^{-4}$ on the validation loss. All experiments are run on a single NVIDIA GPU.

\section{Evaluation Metrics}

Caption quality is measured using four standard automatic metrics computed on the 1{,}000-image test set:

\textbf{BLEU} (Bilingual Evaluation Understudy) measures n-gram precision between generated and reference captions with a brevity penalty. We report BLEU-1 through BLEU-4 \cite{papineni2002}.

\textbf{METEOR} (Metric for Evaluation of Translation with Explicit ORdering) computes an F-score based on unigram alignment between hypothesis and reference, incorporating stemming and synonym matching \cite{banerjee2005}.

\textbf{ROUGE-L} (Recall-Oriented Understudy for Gisting Evaluation -- Longest Common Subsequence) measures the longest common subsequence between generated and reference captions, rewarding fluency at the sentence level \cite{lin2004}.

\textbf{CIDEr} (Consensus-based Image Description Evaluation) computes TF-IDF-weighted n-gram similarity between a generated caption and the set of reference captions, rewarding descriptions that are both accurate and consensus-aligned \cite{vedantam2015}.

Inference latency is measured as the average wall-clock time per image on the test set using greedy decoding.

\subsection{Training Convergence}

Both models begin with similar training losses (3.60 for ResNet-50, 3.58 for ViT-B/16 at epoch 1) and converge to comparable final training losses ($\approx$2.12 and $\approx$1.99 respectively at their last epoch before early stopping). The ViT model achieves a marginally lower best validation loss (2.4665 vs.\ 2.4829), consistent with its slightly higher test-set metric scores. However, the ResNet-50 encoder achieves this near-equivalent convergence in less than 40\% of the wall-clock time required by the ViT encoder.

\subsection{Qualitative Examples}

Both models generate grammatically correct and semantically relevant captions for most test images. For example, on an image of a black-and-white dog running through grass, the ResNet-50 model generates \textit{``a black and white dog is jumping over a green and white toy in a grassy area''}, while the ViT model generates \textit{``a black and white dog runs through the grass with a black and white dog in the background.''}  The ViT caption more accurately describes the global scene layout, whereas the ResNet caption introduces an object not present in the image---illustrating the object hallucination tendency that has been documented in vision-language models \cite{li2023pope}. For images with multiple people or complex backgrounds, the ViT model more consistently captures group activities and scene-level context, which is consistent with ViT's stronger global feature modeling.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Caption Quality}

The ViT-B/16 encoder yields consistently higher BLEU scores than ResNet-50, suggesting that the global contextual representations learned through patch-level self-attention translate into more precise n-gram matches against reference captions. The improvement is modest (e.g., BLEU-4: $+0.0038$) but consistent across all four BLEU variants and CIDEr, indicating a systematic, rather than incidental, advantage. The near-identical METEOR and ROUGE-L scores indicate that both encoders produce captions of comparable fluency and recall, while the ViT model provides slightly higher precision of content words.

\subsection{Computational Trade-offs}

Despite the marginal quality improvement, the ViT encoder introduces substantial computational overhead. The ViT-B/16 pretrained weights are 3.4$\times$ larger than ResNet-50 (330\,MB vs.\ 97.8\,MB), and the per-epoch training time is approximately 2.3$\times$ longer (20.7 vs.\ 9.1\,min), leading to a total training time overhead of 2.5$\times$ (438.5 vs.\ 172.4\,min). This difference arises from the quadratic self-attention complexity over 196 patch tokens in ViT-B/16 compared to the efficient convolutional forward pass of ResNet-50. Importantly, inference latency is nearly equal for both models (42.02 vs.\ 41.33\,ms per image), because at inference time the ViT encoder produces only a single CLS vector that is fed to the same decoder, eliminating any sequence-length overhead.

\subsection{Data Efficiency and Inductive Bias}

Flickr30k provides only 29{,}000 training images, a moderate-scale dataset by modern standards. CNNs embed two forms of inductive bias---locality (each filter covers a limited receptive field) and translation equivariance---that allow them to learn effective visual representations from relatively few examples. ViTs lack these biases and instead learn global spatial relationships purely from data, which is why they typically require much larger datasets (e.g., ImageNet-21k, JFT-300M) or strong regularization to match CNN performance \cite{dosovitskiy2021}. The fact that ViT-B/16 still slightly outperforms ResNet-50 on Flickr30k, despite the small dataset, is attributable to the quality of its ImageNet-1k pretrained representations. However, the narrow margin suggests that ResNet-50's data efficiency advantage significantly limits the gains that ViT can realize on moderate-scale captioning benchmarks.

\subsection{Implications for Deployment}

For applications where caption quality is the primary concern and large computational budgets are available---such as content indexing or accessibility services at scale---the ViT encoder offers a marginal but consistent quality advantage. For resource-constrained deployment scenarios (edge devices, real-time captioning, rapid prototyping), the ResNet-50 encoder provides essentially equivalent quality at substantially lower training cost, with comparable inference latency. These findings suggest that the choice between CNN and ViT encoders should be driven by the available training budget and dataset scale rather than inference requirements alone.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}

This paper presented a controlled comparative study of ResNet-50 and ViT-B/16 visual encoders within an identical encoder-decoder image captioning framework. Both models were trained and evaluated on Flickr30k under matched conditions. The ViT-B/16 encoder achieves marginally higher caption quality on most metrics (BLEU-4: 0.1679 vs.\ 0.1641; CIDEr: 0.3872 vs.\ 0.3868), consistent with its stronger global contextual modeling via patch-level self-attention. However, this improvement comes at the cost of 2.5$\times$ longer training time and a 3.4$\times$ larger encoder footprint, while inference latency remains comparable.

These results confirm that ViT encoders can outperform CNN encoders on global semantic coverage even on moderate-scale datasets, but the advantage is constrained by ViT's lack of CNN inductive biases, which reduces its data efficiency. On a dataset of the scale of Flickr30k, ResNet-50 offers a compelling efficiency--quality trade-off.

Future work will explore: (i) scaling to larger datasets such as MS COCO or CC3M to assess whether ViT's quality advantage widens with more data; (ii) beam search decoding to improve caption fluency for both models; (iii) attention map visualization to interpret which image regions each encoder emphasizes; (iv) fine-tuning BLIP-2 \cite{li2023} as a strong baseline for comparison; and (v) investigating encoder choices specifically for reducing hallucination, guided by evaluation protocols such as POPE \cite{li2023pope}.
